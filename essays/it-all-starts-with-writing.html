It All Starts with Writing

Writing gives you a chance to test the argument before you spend time producing the content.

A lot of content begins as writing, even when the audience never sees the document.

The video had a script. The podcast had an outline. The campaign had a brief. Before any of it was produced, designed, recorded, or published, someone wrote down what they wanted to say.

Trying to explain an idea in writing often changes the idea itself.

You find gaps you hadn't noticed while the idea was still in your head.

I find gaps in my reasoning harder to ignore on the page. In conversation I can move past a weak point before I notice it. Writing gives me a chance to stop and ask whether one thought follows from the last. Other people may do that work through speaking, drawing or making.

Writing makes me check whether one thought actually follows from the last.

That's why the best content starts with a document. A brief. An outline. Words that anyone can react to, challenge, sharpen. Before a single frame is shot or a single slide is designed, someone has to write down what this thing is actually about.

When that step gets skipped, you can feel it. The video meanders. The article says everything and nothing. The campaign looks polished but can't explain itself in one sentence.

The team started producing before agreeing on what it wanted to say.

It's cheaper to change the argument before production begins.

Rewrite a paragraph in seconds. Restructure an outline in minutes. Now try that with a finished video. Writing lets you iterate on the thinking before the expensive part starts.

This is why long-form is one of the most underrated assets in content. Writing 2,000 words gives you plenty of chances to discover what you haven't understood yet.

You have room to examine the reasoning, not just state the conclusion.

A document can be useful even if it never gets published.

Publishing is a bonus. The real value happened the moment you tried to explain your idea clearly enough for someone else to follow it. That's where the sloppy assumption got caught. Where you found a missing connection and made the argument clearer.

It doesn't matter if you write alone, with a colleague, or with AI. What matters is that before the content existed, someone put words on a page and figured out what they were actually trying to say.

A page of writing gives everyone something concrete to respond to.

Start there.
